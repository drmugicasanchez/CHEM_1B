Introduction: Why Nuclear Chemistry Matters

Why Study Nuclear Chemistry?

Nuclear chemistry helps us understand processes that power stars, generate electricity, produce medical images and treatments, and affect the environment. In each case, the nucleus—not just the electrons around it—is changing or releasing energy.

Two ways to release nuclear energy: Fusion joins light nuclei together. Fission splits a heavy nucleus into smaller nuclei. Both processes can release energy because the products are more tightly bound than the original nuclei.

Introduction: Marie Curie and Radioactivity

Marie Curie and the Discovery of Radioactivity

In 1896, Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium compounds emitted penetrating radiation spontaneously. Marie Skłodowska-Curie investigated this phenomenon, helped establish the term radioactivity, and showed that the effect came from atoms themselves.

A short timeline

1896 · Uranium radiation
1896Henri Becquerel observes that uranium compounds emit penetrating radiation spontaneously. This observation begins the modern study of radioactivity.
1898 · Polonium and radium
1898Marie and Pierre Curie report the discovery of polonium and radium and help establish radioactivity as a property of atoms.
1903 · Nobel Prize in Physics
1903Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, and Henri Becquerel share the Nobel Prize in Physics for their work on spontaneous radioactivity.
1911 · Nobel Prize in Chemistry
1911Marie Curie receives the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her work involving radium and polonium, becoming the first person to receive two Nobel Prizes.
Historical perspective: Early researchers did not yet understand the biological hazards of ionizing radiation. The history of radioactivity therefore connects scientific discovery with the later development of radiation protection and ethical responsibility.
Introduction: Benefits, Risks, and Ethics

Implications of Nuclear Chemistry

Nuclear chemistry has important benefits, but using nuclear processes also creates responsibilities involving safety, waste, weapons, and environmental justice.

Potential benefits

  • Low-carbon electricity during normal reactor operation
  • Medical imaging and targeted radiation therapy
  • Tracers used in biology, chemistry, and environmental studies
  • Food safety, sterilization, and industrial measurements

Risks and ethical questions

  • Radiation exposure and the need for reliable safety systems
  • Long-lived radioactive waste and responsibility across generations
  • Accident preparedness, cleanup, and impacts on communities
  • Nuclear weapons, deterrence, proliferation, and humanitarian consequences

The Chernobyl disaster illustrates the consequences of reactor design, operational, and safety failures. These examples are not arguments that nuclear chemistry is simply good or bad; they show why context, safeguards, and ethical decision-making matter.

4.6.1 Isotopes and Nuclear Notation

4.6.1 Isotopes and Nuclear Notation

An isotope is identified by its mass number, A, and atomic number, Z.

AZX     A = protons + neutrons     Z = protons
Mass number, A

The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Atomic number, Z

The number of protons. It identifies the element.

Neutrons

Calculate by subtracting: neutrons = A − Z.

4.6.2 Nuclear Equations

4.6.2 Nuclear Equations

A nuclear equation must conserve both:

Mass number, A

The sum of the superscripts must be the same on both sides.

Atomic number, Z

The sum of the subscripts must be the same on both sides.

Think of A and Z as two separate bookkeeping systems. If one particle is missing, compare the totals to determine it.

4.6.3 Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

4.6.3 Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

Radioactive decay is a first-order process: the fraction that decays during each half-life is constant, but the absolute amount that decays becomes smaller as less material remains.

Each half-life multiplies the remaining amount by 1/2. At five half-lives, 3.125% remains.

Medical-imaging decision

A useful radionuclide must remain active long enough to prepare, administer, and image, but it should not remain radioactive unnecessarily long.

Too short

It may decay before the image is obtained.

Too long

The patient may remain radioactive longer than needed.

Technetium-99m has a 6.0-hour half-life and is useful for several imaging applications because it lasts long enough for imaging while decaying relatively quickly afterward. The best choice also depends on where the radionuclide travels in the body.
Checkpoint 3

Carbon-11 has a half-life of 20.4 min. If the process takes five half-lives, what percentage remains?

3.125% remains. Each half-life halves the amount: 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25 → 3.125.

4.6.4 Radiation and Biological Effects

4.6.4 Radiation and Biological Effects

Radiation differs in how far it travels through matter and how strongly it interacts with material. Select a radiation type to compare penetration and shielding.

Radiation exposure in everyday life

People are exposed to ionizing radiation from natural background sources and, sometimes, from medical procedures. The values below are approximate comparisons; actual doses vary with location, altitude, equipment, procedure, and body size.

ExampleApproximate effective doseWhat it illustrates
One banana0.1 µSvPotassium-40 occurs naturally in food and in the human body.
One cross-country flight20–50 µSvCosmic radiation increases with altitude and flight duration.
One chest X-rayAbout 0.1 mSv = 100 µSvMedical imaging uses ionizing radiation for diagnosis.
Average U.S. annual exposureAbout 6.2 mSv = 6200 µSvA combination of natural background, medical, and consumer sources.
Cosmic radiation: The EPA estimates that cosmic radiation contributes about 5% of the average annual radiation exposure in the United States. Using the 6.2 mSv annual average gives a rough estimate of about 0.3 mSv per year from cosmic radiation; this is an inference, not a universal value.

General pattern:

  • Alpha: low penetration, high ionization over a short distance.
  • Beta: intermediate penetration.
  • Gamma: high penetration; dense shielding is needed.
  • Neutrons: highly penetrating; hydrogen-rich and/or thick shielding is used.
Important distinction: A radiation type can be stopped outside the body but still be dangerous if the radioactive source is inhaled, swallowed, or otherwise brought inside the body.
Checkpoint 4

Which radiation would generally require the greatest external shielding?

Gamma radiation. Gamma rays are highly penetrating electromagnetic radiation, so dense shielding such as lead or thick concrete may be required. Shielding decisions depend on the radiation energy and the source.
